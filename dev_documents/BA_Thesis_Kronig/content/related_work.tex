\section{State-Of-The-Art in OWASP MASTG Reference Apps}
At the time of writing, there are currently 19 MASTG reference apps listed on the OWASP page. To evaluate the state of the art of these apps, it is necessary to examine each one of them in order to identify and categorize their implemented weaknesses. Only 8 of said apps had both available sourcecode as well as some form of documentation of their weaknesses. These were then mapped by the author to their corresponding OWASP MASWE counterparts.


\subsection{Findings}
Across the 8 evaluated OWASP MASTG reference apps, 162 weaknesses where mapped to their respective MASWE counterpart and grouped by their vulnerability category (Storage, Crypto, Auth, etc.).

\subsubsection{Lack of vulnerability documentation}
A reoccurring issue with many of the reference apps is their documentation of the implemented vulnerabilities. Multiple of the reference apps do not have any documentation of their weaknesses at all, which led to their exclusion from this evaluation. Those apps that do have some form of documentation are often incomplete, and almost always only consist of a single list, naming the implemented weaknesses and leaving it at that. None of the apps map the implemented weaknesses to their OWASP MASWE counterparts, despite them being a MASTG reference app, designed for this very purpose. Only one app \todo{add source and specific names of mentioned apps here, just in general less vague than is now.} actually documents what the weaknesses entail, how they are implemented, and how to fix or exploit them. 

Since these apps are meant by OWASP to be educational, it does not suffice to only implement the weaknesses and leave as is. The app developed in this BA thesis aims to fill this hole by not only documenting all implemented weaknesses, but adding documentation for each specific weakness, consisting of where it is found, what lines of code are of relevancy, how to exploit them, aswell as how to fix them.

\section{Literature Review - Academic relevance of OWASP MASTG}
The OWASP MASTG is the industry standard for mobile app security. But what relevancy does it have in the academic world, and what exactly is it used for? To answer this question, a literature review was conducted to evaluate papers on their use of the OWASP MASTG, as well as related OWASP parts, e.g., the MASTG reference apps, the MASWE, and the MASVS.

\subsection{Methodology}
This literature review was conducted by searching for relevant papers that include and deal with the keywords "OWASP" and "MASTG" on google scholar. Only papers written in english have been considered. Furthermore, all evaluated papers have been written in the last two years, with no paper having an older publishing year than 2023. The top 22 papers matching these criteria have been carefully read, with seven of them discarded because their research focus did not match the OWASP MASTG. This left 15 papers, which have then been evaluated for their usage of OWASP MASTG and grouped by similarities.

\subsection{Findings}
Of the 15 papers evaluated, 7 used OWASP MASTG to evaluate real-world android apps based on whether they adhere to the security standard set by OWASP MASVS. 6 papers mapped android app security vulnerabilities to the OWASP MASVS. Three papers made use of the 5-steps penetration testing guide as layed out by OWASP MASTG, in order to conduct penetration tests on android apps. Three papers also used the MASTG reference apps by evaluating vulnerability assessment tools on them in order to verify their accuracy in finding such vulnerabilities. Some \todo{replace "Some" with actual count} of these papers then compared their findings to results found when conducting the same tests on real-world apps. Lastly, four papers mentioned the OWASP MASTG in discussion, either by the authors, comparing the OWASP mobile project to other Mobile Security Frameworks, or by interviewees as a tool used.

\subsubsection{Cryptographic security shortcomings in african mobile financial applications}
One paper \todo{add proper source for paper nr 7 in github repo} used the OWASP MASTG \& MASVS to evaluate 18 android apps belonging to different african financial institutions based on their mobile security. They chose MASVS as a testing framework, since it is specifically designed to "be a guide and checklist for evaluating the security of mobile applications". They applied the strictest level of MASVS, L2, and focused solely on unauthenticated client-side testing, following the black-box testing approach. They did not implement all MASVS tests. Nevertheless, their findings in terms of security shortcomings are quite damning. Some of the worst findings are as follows. All 18 apps allowed for screenshotting of sensitive data. 14 of the apps used outdated and thus insecure cryptographic protocols such as MD5, SHA-1 and DES. 10 apps used security libraries that are outdated and known to have vulnerabilities. Furthermore, eight apps were using insecure random number generation for their cryptographic protocols, and 5 apps have no certificate pinning. This shows a clear and dramatic shortcoming in terms of cryptographic mobile security. All of the above mentioned shortcomings are well documented in the OWASP MASWE, with examples for how to test for such weaknesses described in the MASTG and are part of the verification standard MASVS. This shows that the OWASP mobile security project has a great use case in the real world, and is far from being outdated or redundant. In a  conducted survey by the authors of the study, 78\% of respondents said they avoided implementing security due to a lack of expertise and knowledge about security. By creating a intentionally vulnerable reference app which greatly covers vulnerabilities in the cryptographic sector, this BA thesis fills a hole in the current landscape of available reference apps, which do not cover the CRYPTO section of the MASWE to a sufficient extent. It builds on the issues found and discussed in this paper, by enabling mobile developers to deepen their expertise and knowledge of cryptographic vulnerabilities in a hands-on way.

\subsubsection{Lack of basis for Mutation Testing for android app security testing}
Another paper \todo{add proper source for paper nr 10 as listed in github repo} uses Mutation Testing for android app security testing. To do so, the authors used the OWASP Top 10 Mobile Risks to assess common android app security vulnerabilities, and create mutation operators based on these vulnerabilities. They then perform security tests against the mutants generated by the mutation operators, in the hope that these mutants are detected as vulnerabilities. They do this on 10 android reference apps from the MASTG reference app catalog. They find that four of the eight desired generated mutant operators are not represented among the mutants at all, while two other are heavily overrepresented. To be more specific, all three tapjacking vulnerability mutant operators and the "ImplicitPendingIntent" operator have no representation in the apps, whereas ImproperExport and HardcodedSecret are both heavily overrepresented. This finding further enforces my own findings upon analyzing the implemented vulnerabilities in the reference apps, and can be thus explained. \subsubsection{Security shortcomings in the american mHealth sector}
Another paper \todo{add proper source here for paper nr. 14 as seen in github repo (doctor is spying on you)} used the OWASP Mobile Audit to evaluate 95 apks of real-world apps which are part of the mobile health sector in the USA. The OWASP Mobile Audit is an OWASP project which coexists with other OWASP projects such as the MASTG and MASWE, serving as a tool for static code security analysis of android apps. It "applies static application security testing (SAST) aligned with the MASTG to identify vulnerabilities, insecure configurations, certificate handling issues, and potential malware signatures." The findings were once again quite devastating: these apps dealing with sensitiver user health data had on average 44 critical, 2'194 high, 3'728 medium, 1'735 low, and 1'469 vulnerabilities with no severity assigned. Frequently found vulnerabilities include insecure storage of authentication tokens, absence of certificate pinning, 42 of the 95 apps making use of unencrypted transmission of data containing personal health information over http and reliance on outdated cryptographic primitives. 49.3\% of applications still rely on RSA SHA-1 signatures, exposing them to collision-based exploits such as Janus, which permits undetected APK modification and repackaging. 22 apps were found to be configured to trust all TLS certificates. Furthermore, there 2'252 instances of unnecessarily exported broadcast receivers and 1'232 cases of exported permissions without defined protection levels found. This paper once again underlines the huge shortcoming of apps in the cryptographic sector of mobile security, even when it comes to sensitive user data. All of the found vulnerabilities are once again well documented by the OWASP mobile security project, and need'nt exist anymore.
